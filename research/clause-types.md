# What Goes Wrong: Clause Types That Burn People

Research Agent 2 — pre-PRD validation research for Redline.
Scope: recurring clause types that show up in real complaints/horror stories about contracts, leases, freelance agreements, and terms of service.

Method: 12 web searches + 8 page fetches (within the 12-search / 15-page caps). Stopped once 8+ distinct sourced findings were reached and evidence began to thin out on remaining candidate categories.

---

## Ranked list (by frequency of appearance across sourced evidence)

This ranking is qualitative, based on (a) how many independent sources/complaint types surfaced per category during this search session, and (b) one direct lawyer-blog ranking found (see Finding 8) that explicitly names the clauses that generate the most litigation for small businesses.

1. **Indemnity / hold-harmless clauses** — surfaced across freelance journalism (Poynter), freelance dev/copywriting (ClauseShield), and was independently named as a top litigation driver in a lawyer-blog ranking. Strongest and most consistent signal in this research.
2. **Liability caps (absence of)** — tightly coupled with indemnity in the evidence; freelancer stories consistently pair "no liability cap" with catastrophic financial exposure.
3. **Auto-renewal / cancellation-friction clauses** — major, well-documented pattern (FTC v. LA Fitness, NYC DCWP citywide gym warning, FTC rules on "click to cancel"). Regulatory attention is heavy here, suggesting high real-world volume of complaints even though this session found fewer individual anecdotes than indemnity.
4. **Non-compete clauses** — strong regulatory/news signal (FTC's 2024 rule, 26,000+ public comments, named worker case studies) and independently flagged in the lawyer-blog litigation ranking.
5. **Unilateral/one-sided termination clauses (SaaS & ToS)** — well documented as a structural pattern (no-notice termination, no refund) and flagged in the same lawyer-blog ranking as a top litigation driver, but this search did not turn up a specific first-person complaint/story — evidence here is more "pattern description" than "burned person."
6. **Data privacy overreach / third-party data sharing clauses** — one strong, well-documented case (Flo Health / Meta) with court findings; likely under-represented in this session's search terms rather than in reality — privacy litigation is a large and growing category.
7. **Arbitration clauses / class-action waivers** — clearly a real and heavily litigated pattern (Supreme Court FAA case law, widespread use), but this session found explanatory/legal-analysis sources rather than first-person consumer complaint narratives.
8. **Early termination penalties (leases)** — one concrete anecdote found (Blind/teamblind thread), and it cuts the other way from what people assume: several commenters noted that a penalty is often *not* legally chargeable unless explicitly in the lease, meaning the fight is more often about a clause's absence/ambiguity than its presence.
9. **IP assignment / work-for-hire overreach** — real and reasonably common in freelance-contract advice literature, but this session found explainer/advisory content (why full IP assignment clauses are aggressive) rather than a sourced first-person horror story with a URL to a specific incident.
10. **Fee escalators / hidden fee increases** — evidence was thin. Searches returned only generic contract-clause-template sites (Law Insider boilerplate examples of CPI-linked fee escalators), not complaints or horror stories. Flagged as **evidence gap** below.

## Sourced findings

### 1. Auto-renewal / cancellation-friction clauses
**Example:** The FTC sued Fitness International (LA Fitness, Esporta Fitness, and related brands) alleging the company made cancellation "a confusing maze you have to go through before the gym will honor your cancellation request," including requiring in-person cancellation only on weekdays 9am–5pm with a specific Operations Manager who was frequently "unavailable," and rejecting phone/email cancellation requests. Affects roughly 3.7 million members across 600+ locations.
**Source:** https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/08/la-fitness-made-it-difficult-people-cancel-gym-memberships-ftc-says (also reported via https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/3-7-million-members-stuck-162025511.html)
**Frequency signal:** High. Reinforced by a February 2026 NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection citywide compliance warning to nearly 200 gyms (including PureGym, Planet Fitness, Equinox) over "subscription trap" practices: https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/news/019-26/mamdani-administration-issues-citywide-subscription-trap-compliance-warning-nearly-200-gyms — i.e., this is a pattern regulators are actively pursuing across an entire industry, not an isolated incident.

### 2. Indemnity clauses (freelance/journalism)
**Example:** Forbes contributor Dolia Estevez was sued for defamation after a 2013 piece naming a Mexican official among the "10 most corrupt Mexicans." Forbes's standard freelance contract required contributors to "indemnify Forbes and hold [it] harmless against any liability, cost or expense... incurred as a result of" their work, so Forbes did not defend her the way it would a staff writer; she had to secure pro bono counsel herself. Investigative journalist Barry Yeoman is quoted: "to require a journalist to pony up at the first threat, no matter how baseless, is not only unethical for the publication but completely impractical."
**Source:** https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/indemnity-clauses-leave-freelancers-open-to-lawsuits/
**Frequency signal:** Named explicitly as one of the top three clause types generating litigation for small businesses/contractors in a lawyer-blog ranking (see Finding 8).

### 3. Liability caps (absence of) — freelance/dev contracts
**Example (copywriter):** A copywriter took on a $3,000 project; ad copy triggered an FTC complaint, and the client's indemnification clause made the freelancer personally cover over $80,000 in attorney fees.
**Example (ecommerce developer):** A developer built a store for a $15,000 contract with no liability cap. A payment-processing bug led the client to claim $340,000 in lost holiday sales against the freelancer.
**Source:** https://clauseshield.app/blog/liability-caps-unlimited-liability
**Frequency signal:** Consistently paired with indemnity-clause complaints in freelance-contract advice content; lawyers quoted elsewhere recommend capping liability proportional to fee ("If you're getting $1,000 for the story, you shouldn't have $1 million of liability") — from the same freelance-contract search cluster (Columbia Journalism Review, https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/freelance-contracts.php).

### 4. Non-compete clauses
**Example:** A security guard earning ~$11/hour at a Florida firm resigned over childcare conflicts, took a $15/hour bank job, and was then terminated from the new job after his prior employer sent a non-compete enforcement letter. Separately, a textile-company factory manager saw pay cut after the 2008 financial crisis, was blocked by his non-compete from taking a better job elsewhere, and the ensuing three-year legal battle "wiped out his savings."
**Source:** https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ftc-noncompete-agreement-ban/ (covering the FTC's 2024 non-compete rule and the 26,000+ public comments the FTC received)
**Frequency signal:** Named explicitly as one of the top three clause types generating litigation for small businesses in the same lawyer-blog ranking (Finding 8). Also a distinct federal regulatory action (FTC rulemaking) driven by volume of worker complaints — https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition

### 5. Data privacy overreach / third-party data-sharing clauses
**Example:** Flo Health's privacy policy told users: "We are committed to keeping that trust, which is why our policy as a company is to take every step to ensure that individual user's data and privacy rights are protected," and specifically promised to exclude health/cycle/pregnancy data from third-party sharing. A jury found that between 2016–2019 Flo shared detailed reproductive health data (logged on every app open/interaction) with Meta, Google, AppsFlyer, and Yahoo/Flurry — and that Flo's actual agreements with those companies "allowed them to use the data for their own purposes, completely unrelated to services provided in connection with the App." A California jury found Meta intentionally accessed this data without proper consent.
**Source:** https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2025/08/meta-accessed-womens-health-data-from-flo-app-without-consent-says-court
**Frequency signal:** Moderate in this search session (one strong case found), but this is likely an undercount — data-privacy class actions are a large and active legal category (see general tracker: https://thelyonfirm.com/class-action/data-privacy/). Flagged as an area worth deeper dedicated research beyond this session's scope.

### 6. Arbitration clauses / class-action waivers
**Example/explainer:** "A consumer arbitration clause can force a dispute out of court and into private arbitration, sometimes with strict notice rules or deadlines... Class actions are a vital legal tool, allowing many individuals with small but similar claims — like hidden fees or data privacy violations — to band together and challenge corporate misconduct. Without this mechanism, most consumers cannot realistically pursue claims, effectively granting corporations immunity for widespread but low-value harms."
**Source:** https://blog.referu.ai/legal-information-by-practice-area/consumer-protection-law/consumer-arbitration-clauses
**Frequency signal:** Structurally significant (Supreme Court has held the FAA makes most class-action waivers enforceable per https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12764), but this session did not surface a specific named individual's complaint/story tied to a URL — evidence here is explanatory rather than anecdotal. Treat frequency ranking as provisional.

### 7. Early termination penalties (leases)
**Example:** A landlord (via a Blind/teamblind thread) asked what penalty to charge a tenant who gave 30 days' notice to leave early. Commenters pushed back: "you cannot charge penalty. you can charge the rent until you can find a new tenant," and noted the landlord must "make reasonable effort to rent out your property again as soon as possible" (mitigation duty) rather than collect a flat penalty, and that without specific penalty language already in the lease, no termination fee can legally be imposed.
**Source:** https://www.teamblind.com/post/lease-termination-penalty-by-tenant-sp1p00sw
**Frequency signal:** Single anecdote found in this session. Notably, the dispute pattern here is about the *ambiguity/absence* of an early-termination clause as much as an overreaching one — worth noting for Redline's framing (missing protective language is itself a risk to flag, not just aggressive language).

### 8. Ranking source — most litigated clause types for small businesses
**Finding:** A small-business litigation lawyer blog explicitly ranks three clause types as the ones creating the most litigation: **(1) Indemnity clauses** — "can force a small business to bear responsibility for damages it never caused or anticipated," especially with vague language like "any and all claims"; **(2) Non-compete clauses** — courts "scrutinize them closely," and overly broad restrictions "are unlikely to survive legal challenge and create enforcement costs"; **(3) Termination clauses** — "often at the center of disputes when relationships end," especially when contracts lack clear notice periods, final-payment timelines, or property-return terms.
**Source:** https://www.aikenslawfirm.com/the-contract-clauses-that-create-the-most-litigation-for-small-businesses/
**Frequency signal:** This is a direct ranking-type source (exactly the kind of signal requested), independently corroborating indemnity and non-compete as top offenders found elsewhere in this research, and adding **termination clauses** (notice/exit terms) as a third major category.

## What I could not find (evidence gaps)

- **Fee escalators / hidden fee increases:** Searches returned only generic contract-clause template/boilerplate sites (Law Insider examples of CPI-linked fee-increase clauses) with no complaint narratives or horror stories attached. I could not source a real complaint for this category within the search budget — treat as **unverified/thin** rather than confirmed low-frequency.
- **Unilateral/one-sided SaaS termination clauses:** Confirmed as a real structural pattern (no-notice termination, no-refund terms; one ToS fairness dataset explicitly flags "the company reserves the right to terminate a user's account at any time for any reason without notice" as "clearly unfair") but I did not find a specific first-person complaint story with a URL — only pattern/explainer sources and boilerplate clause libraries.
- **IP assignment / work-for-hire overreach:** Well-covered from an advisory/explainer angle (why full IP assignment clauses are aggressive for freelancers, and why most freelance deliverables don't legally qualify as "work made for hire"), but no sourced first-person horror story with a specific dollar figure or outcome was found in the search budget, unlike indemnity/liability-cap clauses where concrete dollar-figure stories were found.
- **Arbitration/class-action waivers — first-person anecdotes:** Legal/structural sourcing is strong, but no specific "here's what happened to me" complaint story was found and verified within budget.
- I did not exhaust all 12 searches evenly across categories — once indemnity/liability-cap and non-compete/auto-renewal produced strong, verifiable, dollar-figure-backed stories, remaining budget was spent trying (with partial success) to backfill weaker categories (data privacy, leases, non-compete testimonials) rather than continuing to broaden into untested categories (e.g., I did not specifically search "moving company" or "wedding vendor" contract complaints, "background check"/"credit report" ToS clauses, or "timeshare" contracts, which are also known problem areas anecdotally but were out of scope given the search cap).
